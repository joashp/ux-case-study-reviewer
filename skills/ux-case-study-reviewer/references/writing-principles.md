# Writing principles and what hiring managers want

## Writing principles (when no code is named)

Pull only the ones the draft actually violates; don't recite the list.

- **Lead with the problem, not the solution.** The reader must feel what was broken before seeing a mockup.
- **Show the messy middle.** Wrong turns and killed options prove real decisions. Polish hides thinking.
- **Every decision needs a "because."** If a choice can't finish "I did this because ___," cut it or justify it.
- **Quantify or honestly characterize impact.** Numbers first ("reduced task time by 40%"); otherwise be honest ("support tickets on this flow dropped noticeably"). Always end with what changed.
- **Write for a 30-second skim, then reward the deep read.** Headers and bolded lines should tell the story alone.
- **One case study, one clear role.** Vague "we" reads as doing less. Be precise about what was theirs.
- **Cut the favorite sentence.** The line they're proudest of is often the most self-indulgent.

## What makes hiring managers lean in

Push the user toward these, using the prompts verbatim or close to it.

**Trade-offs.** Junior: "I chose A because it was better." Senior: "I chose A, knowing I gave up B, because here B mattered less." Have them name the tension and its cost.
> "Take this design decision and reframe it as a trade-off. What did I give up by choosing this, and why was that acceptable here?"

**The plot twist.** The moment data contradicted an assumption ("we assumed X, research showed Y, we changed course"). Turn it into a narrative beat instead of burying it.
> "Where in this project did something surprise me or prove my initial assumption wrong? Help me turn that moment into a clear turning point in the story."

**Rough feedback.** The hardest thing to fake. Help them write a real stumble (killed design, bad usability test, stakeholder pushback): the feedback, their first reaction, what they learned, what changed. Don't sand off the discomfort.
> "Help me write about a moment I got hard feedback on this project. I want it to show resilience and growth without sounding defensive or self-pitying."
> Warn them: manufactured humility ("my only weakness is I care too much") reads badly. Use a real stumble with a real lesson.
